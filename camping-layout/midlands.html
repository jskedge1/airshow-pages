Midlands Air Festival

Over 100 hot air balloons, an afternoon air display and the Friday Nightfire

Midlands Air Festival is one of the most unusual days out in the air show calendar. Balloons fill the sky at dawn and dusk, aircraft fly in the afternoon, and on the Friday night the balloons glow to music before the fireworks. Here’s how I would plan your visit to Ragley, and I hope it helps you and your family.

The festival at a glance

Midlands Air Festival is something a bit different. It mixes a proper air display with over a hundred hot air balloons, all in the grounds of Ragley Hall, a grand country house near Alcester in Warwickshire. You get warbirds, jets and aerobatics in the afternoon, and balloons filling the sky in the early morning and evening.

The festival runs over three days, from Friday 4 to Sunday 6 June 2027. Friday is the big evening, called Nightfire, with balloons glowing to music, aircraft carrying fireworks on their wings and a firework finale. Saturday and Sunday have afternoon air displays, with balloon mass ascents at the start and end of the day, weather permitting.

I would suggest thinking about what you most want to see before you book. If it’s the balloons, I think an early start or a Friday evening is a must. If it’s the aircraft, the Saturday and Sunday afternoons are where the main flying is.

Jack’s tipTickets are advance only, with none sold on the gate. I’d book before you travel, and please don’t turn up without one.

    Ragley’s Story

    I always think a day out means more when you know where you’re standing. The balloons take off from the parkland of a house that has stood here for over three hundred years, and the festival itself is a newer chapter in the estate’s long story.

    1. 1680

      A New House

      Work on the house began in 1680 for Edward Conway, the 1st Earl of Conway. It was designed by William Hurlbert, with the scientist and architect Robert Hooke reworking the plans. The Earl died in 1683, before it was finished.

    2. 1750s

      Gibbs And Capability Brown

      In the middle of the 18th century the house was made fit to live in for the 1st Earl of Hertford. James Gibbs reworked the interior, and Capability Brown remodelled the grounds before 1758, sweeping away the old formal gardens. Much of his parkland survives, and it’s the landscape you’ll be watching the balloons from, which I think is rather lovely.

    3. 1780

      James Wyatt

      Around 1778 to 1783 the architect James Wyatt made more changes to the interior, and the house took on much of the look it still has today.

    4. 1914

      Two World Wars

      In both the First and Second World Wars the house was used as a military hospital. Two years before the first war, in 1912, the estate’s trustees had even recommended it be pulled down.

    5. 1956

      Saved And Opened Up

      Hugh Seymour, the 8th Marquess of Hertford, inherited Ragley in 1940 and fought to save it after the war. Between 1956 and 1958 the house was refurbished and became one of the first stately homes to open to the public.

    6. 1983

      The Great Mural

      In 1983 the artist Graham Rust finished The Temptation, a huge mural on the south staircase. The house has also appeared on screen, including the Doctor Who episode The Girl in the Fireplace and the fourth series of The Crown.

    7. 2018

      The First Festival

      The first Midlands Air Festival was held at Ragley in 2018, with a Friday evening Nightfire and two days of flying. On the Sunday, 72 hot air balloons took to the sky together.

    8. 2021

      Back After The Pandemic

      The pandemic pushed the festival into 4 to 6 June 2021, and all three days sold out.

    9. Today

      Over A Hundred Balloons

      The festival has grown from a modest first year into one of the biggest dates in the calendar, with around a hundred balloons lifting off on the Friday evening in 2026 and a temporary runway in front of the crowd line. In 2027 it returns from Friday 4 to Sunday 6 June.

      Jack’s tipThe house itself is now kept for events and filming rather than everyday visits, so I would suggest enjoying the view of it across Capability Brown’s parkland while you wait for the balloons.

    The Balloons

    For me the balloons are what make this festival so special. Over a hundred of them come to Ragley, with up to a hundred taking off together in a mass ascent, plus special shapes you won’t believe can fly. Here’s when to see them, what happens if the weather says no, and where I would stand.

    Why So Early And So Late?

    Hot air balloons only fly in the early morning and in the evening. That’s when there’s no thermal activity, the rising pockets of warm air that build up on a sunny day and make a balloon hard to control. So the balloons wrap around the air display, with the flying in the afternoon and the balloons at either end of the day.

    The festival plans four mass ascents, from the Friday evening to the Sunday morning, all weather permitting.

    What’s the weather doing?

    A Mass Ascent

    The balloons inflate on the field, the burners roar, and up to a hundred of them lift off and drift away with the breeze. They can be in the air for a long time, so I'd suggest you keep looking up as they float over the countryside.

    A Tethered Or Burner Display

    The pilots decide on the day if it’s safe. If the balloons can’t fly freely, the festival team will try to put on a tethered display, with the balloons held to the ground, or a burner display instead. So I think it’s still worth heading over.

    The Fireglow

    Part of Nightfire. Up to 15 balloons fire their burners in time to music, lighting up the dark like giant lanterns, while other burners send flames around 20 metres into the sky. It finishes with a giant firework display, and children love to see it.

    Ways to arrive

    Driving, premium and accessible parking, the train, the bus, a bike or on foot. Times and fares change, so I would suggest checking them before you travel.

    Is parking free?
    Standard parking is free and included with your ticket. Premium parking is bought when you book, and it has its own entry gate, its own ticket lane and parking in the event field. Disabled parking is free, but you choose it as a ticket when you book.
    Is there parking for Blue Badge holders?
    Yes. There’s a signed disabled car park directly opposite the main event field, with accessible toilets. You’ll need a disabled parking pass, which you choose when you buy your ticket, then just display your Blue Badge on the day. The stewards will help you when you arrive.
    Should I follow my sat nav?
    Only until you’re close. I would head for Alcester, then follow the yellow event signs once you see them, rather than your sat nav.
    Can I come by bus on a Sunday?
    The Diamond 247 between Redditch and Evesham stops right by the Ragley Hall main entrance. Diamond’s current timetable has no Sunday service on the 247, so on the Sunday I would plan another way to get there, and check the Diamond Bus timetable again closer to the festival.
    How far is the walk from the entrance?
    It’s about 1.5 miles from the main entrance to the house, so I’d suggest you wear comfortable shoes. From the main car park it’s between about 250 metres and a kilometre to the event field.

    Where to stay

    With dawn balloon ascents, I have found staying nearby makes the early starts so much easier. You can camp on site, or stay in Alcester, Stratford upon Avon, Redditch or the villages around them. I would suggest booking early, as rooms go quickly on festival weekend.

      Get creative with the balloons

      Here’s where I would start. They’re starting points rather than fixed settings, so I would adjust for the light on the day.

      Ideas to try

      • Dawn balloons. Soft, low light and plenty of colour. I have found a wide lens shows the scale of a mass ascent. I'd suggest you keep the ISO at 1200 or below, and steady yourself or use a tripod as the light is low.
      • The inflation. It's a good idea to get in close while the balloons fill up on the grass. A wide lens looking into the envelope, or the burner flame lighting it from inside, makes a very different picture.
      • Propeller aircraft. Around 1/160 to 1/320 of a second gives you a nice prop blur. Helicopters need slower, around 1/125 down to 1/60, as the rotor blades turn a lot slower. I'd pan smoothly and slow down gradually as you get more confident.
      • Jets. Around 1/1000 to 1/2000 of a second freezes fast jets. I would use single point focus, and leave a little room in the frame for cropping.
      • Nightfire. A tripod is your friend for the Fireglow and fireworks. The balloons barely move during the Fireglow, so you don’t need a fast shutter. I’d keep the ISO at 1200 or below and let the tripod do the work.
      • Avoid machine gun. With balloons especially, there’s no need to hold the shutter down. They move slowly, so you might want to take your time, think about your composition and wait for the right moment.

      Starting settings

      Pick what’s flying and the weather, and you’ll get a starting point. I’ve kept it to no faster than 1/2000 and no higher than ISO 1200, so the files stay clean and the shutter stays realistic for the subject. I would check your histogram after the first pass and adjust from there.

      Food, facilities & what to expect

      There’s a food village on site, and you’re welcome to bring your own food and drink too. For the rules on chairs, gazebos, BBQs and dogs, take a look at .

      Food & drink

      There’s a range of food and drink in the food village, so you won’t go hungry. In 2026 there was a good choice for different diets, including vegetarian food, plus sweet treats like pick and mix and churros.

      • Food village. You’ll find hot food, snacks and drinks around the site. Queues build at lunchtime, so I would suggest eating a little earlier or later.
      • Bringing your own food. Food and drink are allowed, so I think a picnic is a great way to save money, particularly for families. No BBQs or open fires, though.
      • Early and late. If you’re there for the dawn balloons or staying for Nightfire, I would pack a flask and some snacks for the hours either side of the main day.

      What to expect on the day

      Toilets

      You’ll find accessible toilets right around the site, including in the disabled car park.

      Seating & shade

      There’s no seating provided, so you might want to bring your own chairs. Gazebos aren’t allowed, but small pop up tents are.

      Leaving and coming back

      You can leave and come back in. Just get your hand stamped on the way out.

      What to wear

      I would wear country walking clothes and shoes, with layers for a chilly dawn and a torch for after dark.

      Accessibility

      Disabled parking. A signed disabled car park sits directly opposite the main event field, with accessible toilets. I'd suggest you choose a disabled parking pass when you buy your ticket and display your Blue Badge on the day.

      Carers. Carer tickets are available for carers and companions over 16. If someone under 16 needs a professional carer, it's a good idea to contact the organisers.

      The ground. A mix of tarmac roads and grass, so it can be hard going in a wheelchair after rain. Please think about this when you plan.

      Guide dogs are welcome. Other dogs aren’t allowed anywhere on site, including the car parks.

      Dogs
      Dogs and other pets aren’t allowed anywhere on site, including the car parks. Guide dogs are welcome, so please see the rules before you go.
      If it’s windy or wet
      The balloons only fly in calm air. If they can’t fly freely, the team will try a tethered or burner display instead. I’d pack a waterproof whatever the forecast says, and check the festival’s social media on the morning.
      After dark
      On the Friday the festival finishes after dark, so I would bring a torch and sturdy shoes for the walk back to your car or tent.
