The shows at a glance

IWM Duxford is a real Battle of Britain airfield south of Cambridge, and home to the largest collection of airworthy historic aircraft in Europe. Every show ticket also gets you into the museum hangars, so I would plan time for both.

Duxford runs two Big two day Air Shows, the Summer Air Show in June and the Battle of Britain Air Show in September. These are the busy ones. You get the largest line-ups, a flightline walk in the morning, traders and food vendors, and a free shuttle bus from Cambridge. Parking at these two needs a permit booked with your ticket.

Around them sit three themed Flying Days, a Flying Evening built around sunset and a Flying Finale to close the season. They’re smaller and calmer, parking needs no booking, and the Flying Days are free for IWM members. If you are not sure about going to one of the big Duxford air shows, then why not try one of their Flying Days first? This will give you a taster of what the bigger shows are like, with a quieter day and some of the same aircraft.

The museum

Every Duxford show ticket also gets you into the museum, with hangars full of historic aircraft and the American Air Museum. It’s a real Battle of Britain airfield, so you’re walking through history before the first aircraft starts up.

It’s a great way for your kids to learn about the different roles in the armed forces, past and present, and the hangars are the best shade on the site on a hot day. I would suggest planning a couple of hours for the museum around the flying.

    My view

    There are only a few places that match Duxford for historic aircraft. Watching a Spitfire scramble from the same grass it flew from in 1940 is something special. The hangars also give you the opportunity to look around and explore the history of the airfield, and to get up close to aircraft that are rare in the UK, like the SR-71 Blackbird, or take a look round the RAF workhorse that was the Tornado GR4. I would advise the Battle of Britain Air Show if you love warbirds, and the Summer Air Show for a broader mix. If what excites you is fast jets and military noise, I would suggest pairing Duxford with RIAT or the RAF Air Show at Cosford.

    Duxford’s Story

    I always think a day at Duxford means more when you know where you’re standing. The airfield was here long before the museum, and almost every part of it has a story, from the first Spitfires in RAF service to the American fighter pilots who flew from here in the war.

    1. 1917

      Building An Airfield

      Work on the airfield started in October 1917, and the first units arrived in March 1918 to train pilots for the First World War. Some of the hangars you walk past today date from those very first years.

    2. 1923

      A Fighter Station

      On 1 April 1923 Duxford became a fighter station, and 19 Squadron formed here the same month. That squadron goes on to play a big part in the next chapter.

    3. 1938

      The First Spitfires

      On 4 August 1938, 19 Squadron received its first Spitfire, K9789, only the third one ever built. It made Duxford the first home of the Spitfire in squadron service, which is why seeing one fly here feels so special.

    4. 1940

      The Battle Of Britain

      In July 1940, 310 Squadron formed here with Hurricanes, flown by Czechoslovak pilots who had escaped to Britain. Douglas Bader led 242 Squadron as part of the Duxford “Big Wing”, and on 15 September Duxford’s squadrons helped turn back the raids on London.

      This is the story the Battle of Britain Air Show in September is built around, so I would suggest that show if this part grabs you.

    5. 1943

      Station 357

      In April 1943 the US Army Air Forces took over, and Duxford became Station 357, home of the 78th Fighter Group. They flew P-47 Thunderbolts, then swapped to P-51 Mustangs in December 1944. Their last mission was on 25 April 1945, and they left that October. It’s a big reason the American Air Museum is here today.

    6. 1945

      Into The Jet Age

      The RAF took Duxford back on 1 December 1945. A concrete runway went down between 1949 and 1951, and by 1957 Javelins and Hunters were flying from here. On 1 August 1961 a Meteor made the last take off, and RAF Duxford closed.

    7. 1968

      Lights, Camera, Explosion

      In June 1968 one of the First World War hangars was blown up for the film Battle of Britain. The Spanish built Buchóns that played the German fighters in that film are still a familiar sight in the Duxford skies.

    8. 1977

      Saved For Flying

      In 1977 Cambridgeshire County Council, IWM and the Duxford Aviation Society bought the runway together. The new M11 meant the runway had to be shortened, which is why the motorway runs so close to the end of it. In 2008 IWM agreed to buy the runways and around 146 acres of grass, securing the airfield for flying.

    9. Today

      A Living Airfield

      Today it’s a working airfield as well as a museum, with historic aircraft looked after, restored and flown from the same hangars. When you watch a Spitfire take off from the grass here, you’re seeing it on the airfield where it all began.

      Jack’s tipI would suggest taking a slow walk along the line of hangars before the crowds build. Little details like the old doors and the control tower make the history feel very real.

    Ways to arrive

    Driving, the train and the free Air Show shuttle, local buses and taxis. Times and fares do change, so I would check them before you travel.

    Do I need to book parking?
    Yes for the Summer Air Show and the Battle of Britain Air Show. Every car needs a parking permit, booked when you buy your tickets, and spaces are limited, so you can’t turn up and park without one. At the Flying Days, the Flying Evening and the Flying Finale, parking is on site with no booking needed. Motorbikes park free in the visitor centre car park with no permit.
    Is there parking for Blue Badge holders?
    Yes. Anyone with mobility issues can park in the main car park, but proof needs to be provided to park there, and staff will be on hand to answer any questions. At the two big Air Shows, I would book an accessible parking permit with your ticket and bring your Blue Badge (or international equivalent) on the day. If you haven’t been before, you’ll see a Hurricane on a pole at the main gate, and this is the way in for anyone with a disabled badge or mobility issues. There’s a wheelchair accessible shuttle bus from the accessible parking to the main showground.
    Should I follow my sat nav?
    Only until you’re close. IWM runs a special traffic plan on Air Show weekends, so once you leave the M11 at junction 10, I would follow the event signs and the marshals instead.
    Will there be traffic?
    Yes, on the big Air Show days. Traffic builds on the M11 slip roads and the A505 from about 08:00, so arriving around 07:00 makes a real difference. It’s worth checking National Highways for road works before you set off.
    How do I get home easily?
    It’s very common for leaving a Duxford Air Show to take a long time. I would either leave before the flying display ends or, if you’re able to, stay on, take a look around the static displays and the other museum hangars, and grab some food while the rush leaves. The site stays open until 19:00 at the big Air Shows. If you’re on the free shuttle, the last bus leaves at 18:30.

      Help On Show Days

      Big show days are busy, and little things can catch you out, like a lost ticket, no cashpoint or losing each other in the crowd. Here’s what I would do if something goes wrong, plus quick answers to the questions I see people ask most before every show.

      In an emergency, call 999. For anything else on the day, I would find the nearest steward or member of IWM staff. They’re there to help.

      If Something Goes Wrong

      Lost Each Other

      I would agree a meeting point as soon as you arrive, like the control tower or a hangar. For younger children, I would advise writing your phone number on their wrist. If you can’t find them, please tell a steward straight away.

      Feeling Unwell

      If you tell the nearest steward or member of staff, they can get help to you. On a hot day, I see people caught out by the sun on the crowd line, so drink plenty and use the hangars for shade.

      Lost Your Ticket

      I would check your junk folder for emails from Digitickets first. If there is still nothing, you can use IWM’s contact form, choose Tickets then Air Shows, and include your booking reference.

      Need Water

      There are free water points around the site, so I would bring a refillable bottle and top it up through the day.

      Paying

      There’s no cashpoint on site. The visitor centre shop and the catering take card or cash, but the shop by the control tower and the American Air Museum are card only, and some traders are cash only.

      Phone And Wi-Fi

      Free Wi-Fi works in and around the visitor centre, cafés, shops and museum, but it can be slow with the crowds. I would suggest downloading your tickets before you leave home.

      Bad Weather

      The hangars are your best shelter from rain or strong sun. Historic aircraft are sensitive to wind and low cloud, so some displays may change or be cut short.

      Finding Your Car

      The car parks are big grass fields and they all look the same at 18:00. I would drop a pin on your phone’s map when you park, as it saves a lot of wandering.

      Quick Answers

      What time should I get there?

      Car parks open at 07:00 and entry starts at 08:00 at the Big Air Shows. IWM advises arriving before 11:00 so you don’t miss anything, but I would suggest earlier if you want a front row pitch and time on the flightline.

      Can I bring chairs and a blanket?

      Yes, folding chairs and picnic blankets are welcome. Step ladders and stools are not allowed.

      Can I bring a windbreak or a tent?

      A windbreak is fine as long as it’s properly secured and not next to the display barriers. Small tents are allowed if they don’t block anyone’s view. Large tents and gazebos are not allowed.

      Can I bring my own food?

      Yes, packed lunches and picnics are fine. Barbecues aren’t allowed. There are also cafés on site and food vendors across the showground.

      Can I bring my dog?

      Only guide dogs and assistance dogs are allowed, and that includes the car parks. Please leave pet dogs at home, as it gets far too hot in a car.

      Is there a cashpoint?

      No, there isn’t a cashpoint on site. I would bring a little cash for the traders that don’t take cards.

      Can the kids bring scooters, balls or balloons?

      No, I’m afraid children’s scooters, including electric ones, and balls and balloons aren’t allowed.

      Can I fly my drone?

      No, drones aren’t allowed, so please leave it at home.

      How much is a programme?

      Programmes are £8 in advance or £10 on the day while stocks last. If you bought one in advance, you just show your email confirmation at a collection point.

      Is my museum entry included?

      Yes. Your Air Show ticket covers the museum, the hangars, the showground, the flightline before the flying and the flying itself. Parking isn’t included, so I would book a permit if you’re driving.

      Where can the children play?

      The Weston Play Zone is a free outdoor play area next to The Spitfire Café, open 10:00 to 18:00.

      For me this is one of the best parts of a Duxford Air Show, and lots of first timers don’t realise it’s there. Before the flying starts, you can walk along the flightline and get right up close to the aircraft taking part, while the pilots and ground crew get them ready to fly.

      5. Be out by noonThe flightline closes at around 12 noon. I would use the gap for food and the toilet before the flying display starts.

      Good Manners On The Flightline

      Look, don’t touch

      These are irreplaceable historic aircraft, so please stay behind the barriers and only go closer if a crew member invites you.

      Keep little ones close

      Aircraft, fuel bowsers and vehicles move around in the morning. I would hold hands with little ones near the barriers.

      Ears ready

      If an engine run starts close by, it’s loud. I would advise having ear defenders handy for children.

      Follow the stewards

      When it’s time to clear the flightline, they’ll let you know. I would head back to your pitch in good time.

      For photographersThe flightline is where I get my close detail shots, like cockpits, nose art and engines. I would walk the line both ways and look for side light on the aircraft. A wider lens works really well here.

      Inside the show

      1. 1
        Crowd line centre

        Opposite show centre, with the commentary and the Visitor Centre food close by. Light: into the sun around midday, sun behind your right shoulder from about 16:00.

      2. 2
        East end fence, by the grass runway

        The Tank Bank, west end

      Around the airfield

      1. 5
        Farm track, in line with the runway

        Off the A505 just after the Thriplow turn coming from Royston. Good for approaches to 06 and take offs from 24, mostly undersides. It’s usually closed to cars on show days, so you’ll need to walk or cycle in. No parking, and the track can be very muddy.

      2. 6
        Road along the south side

        Pull offs along the road past the T-junction at the end of the farm track. A good view over the airfield and some aircraft pass close on the downwind leg, but it’s too far for most photos.

      3. 7
        Public footpath towards the M11

        A public footpath round the field edge from Duxford village towards the 24 end. Good for approaches to 24, but it’s under the approach path, so I wouldn’t say it’s the safest place to stand. There’s no parking on the village road on show days.

      Spots outside the fence are not official viewing areas, so there’s no commentary, toilets or food, and you can be moved on. Please never enter fields without the farmer’s permission, and drones aren’t allowed.

      Get creative on the airfield

      Duxford gives you a real Battle of Britain airfield as your backdrop, with hangars, the control tower and grass under the aircraft. The crowd faces the sun around midday, so I would suggest the flightline walk and the hangars in the morning, side light in the early afternoon, and the best flying shots from about 16:00.

      Ideas to try

      • I would slow the shutter for the warbirds. 1/160 to 1/250 gives a proper prop disc, so pan smoothly and take a few frames each pass.
      • I would head to the Tank Bank at the west end for take offs and aircraft taxiing within feet of you.
      • I would wait for the Big Wing and the formation passes, and zoom out a bit so the formation fills the frame with some sky around it.
      • You can try including the hangars or the control tower now and then, so the photo says Duxford.
      • Take a look at the photographers’ map in the Where to watch section to pick your spot for the light.

      There’s very little shade on the crowd line. The hangars are great for a cool down, and packed lunches are allowed.

      Leave the scooter

      Children’s scooters, balls and balloons aren’t allowed, so I would leave them in the car or at home.

      Jack’s tipIf your children aren’t sure about aviation yet, the morning is the best bit for them. Getting close to the aircraft on the flightline, then the hangars and the play area, usually wins them over before the flying even starts.

      Food, facilities & what to expect

      IWM Duxford has a few places to eat and drink around the museum. If you’re after a hot meal, a quick sandwich or just a coffee, you’ll find something during your visit.

      Food & drink

      There are three places to eat in the museum, and during the big Air Shows you’ll find extra outdoor food vendors across the site, selling burgers and hot dogs through to snacks, ice cream and drinks. Licensed bars serve craft beer, cider, wine and soft drinks. Locations and availability may vary between events.

      • Spitfire Café ↗ Near the main Visitor Centre and museum entrance. This is handy when you first arrive or just before you leave, with hot meals, sandwiches, cakes, snacks and hot and cold drinks.
      • 19 Squadron Restaurant ↗ In the middle of the museum, near the historic hangars. I would suggest it for a lunch break while you look round the hangars nearby, with cooked meals and drinks.
      • American Air Museum Café ↗ At the American Air Museum, towards the western end of the site. It’s an easy stop while you’re in the American Air Museum, with lighter bites like sandwiches, cakes and hot and cold drinks.
      • Bringing your own food. Picnics are welcome and a great way to save money, particularly for families. I would bring plenty of drinking water and a few snacks, especially if you plan to spend most of the day watching the flying. Free water refill points are around the site.

      My advice: food outlets get really busy during the big Air Shows, especially around lunchtime. I would consider eating a little earlier or later to avoid the queues. Opening times and food options may vary, so I would recommend checking the official IWM Duxford website before your visit.

      Getting around

      Duxford's own shuttle

      It’s around 700 metres from the shop to the American Air Museum. Duxford has its own shuttle that takes people up and down the airfield, which is a real help with little legs or tired ones. At the big Air Shows a wheelchair accessible shuttle bus also runs from the accessible parking to the main showground, and on museum days a free site vehicle with room for a manual wheelchair is available on request.

      What to expect on the day

      Toilets

      Accessible toilets across the site, with extra units and baby changing at the Air Shows. A Changing Places toilet with a shower is in Hangar 1, opened with a Radar key from staff or volunteers.

      Shade & weather

      There’s very little shade on the crowd line. Small shade tents for babies are fine if they don’t block views, but large tents and gazebos aren’t. The hangars give shelter from sun and rain.

      Paying

      The museum cafés take cards. There’s no cashpoint on site, and some traders only take cash, so I would bring a little with you.

      Banned items

      Barbecues, drones, step ladders and stools, large tents and gazebos, children's scooters, balls and balloons, flags and banners, replica weapons and art supplies.

      Accessibility

      Accessible parking. Anyone with mobility issues can park in the main car park, but proof needs to be provided to park there. Staff will be on hand to answer any questions. At the two big Air Shows, I would book an accessible parking permit with your ticket and bring your Blue Badge (or international equivalent) on the day.

      Finding it. If you haven’t been before, you’ll see a Hurricane on a pole at the main gate. This is the way in for anyone with a disabled badge or mobility issues. The main car park for the shows is signposted, and the walk is only a few minutes.

      Viewing. There's a wheelchair viewing area for visitors and their personal assistants, with accessible toilets nearby, and an accessible quiet zone near the American Air Museum, away from the main crowd line. The flightline is on level tarmac.

      Mobility scooters and wheelchairs. At the big Air Shows, electric scooters (£35) and manual wheelchairs (£15) can be booked in advance through Event Mobility. On museum days, wheelchairs and scooters are free to borrow, but numbers are limited, so I would book ahead.

      Assistance dogs are welcome. Other dogs aren’t allowed on site at the Air Shows, including in the car parks.

      Dogs
      Only guide and assistance dogs are allowed at the Air Shows. This includes the car parks, so please leave pet dogs at home.
      Lost children
      I would agree a meeting point when you arrive, such as the control tower or a hangar, and write your phone number on younger children’s wrists.
      If it rains
      Historic aircraft are sensitive to wind and low cloud, so some displays may be shortened or cancelled. The hangars and the American Air Museum give shelter. I would check IWM Duxford’s social media on the morning, and pack a waterproof whatever the forecast says.
      Programmes
      Programmes cost £8 in advance or £10 on the day while stock lasts. Pre-ordered items are collected on site by showing your email confirmation.
