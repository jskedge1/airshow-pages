Rainy Day Plan

Staying Dry And Still Seeing The Flying

I have sat through plenty of wet airshows, and some of them were among my favourites. A grey, drizzly day very rarely stops a show, so pack for rain, set off anyway and make a plan for the quiet bits.

This is the question I get asked more than any other. Most of the time the answer is yes.

Pilots can fly in rain. What they look at is how high the cloud is and how far they can see. A shower that passes through in twenty minutes barely changes anything, while a steady layer of low grey cloud with murky visibility can keep the fast jets on the ground even if it isn't actually raining.

The display teams have a plan for this. With lots of clear sky above them you get the full show with the loops. When the cloud is lower they fly a rolling show, with rolls and wingovers kept under the cloud. Lower again and it becomes a flat show, which is a series of fast passes and formation changes along the crowd line. It is loud and close, and great for photos.

Wind is the other one to watch. The very old and lightweight aircraft, like some of the Edwardian machines at Shuttleworth, need almost calm conditions, so a breezy day can ground them even under a blue sky.

Grey skies, still flying. The jets just stay lower.

Want the numbers? The cloud heights for each shape of display, the legal minimums and a slider to test your own forecast are all in my Weather and will it fly guide. I would read that one the night before.
I would suggest checking the show's Facebook page or X account from about 09:00 on the day. Changes to the flying get posted there first, and the commentator will keep you updated once you are in.

Unfortunately the weather on the day will be what it will be, so plan accordingly. If it's fresh, dress in layers to keep warm.

The forecast says

Light showers

Go, and enjoy it

This is a normal British airshow day. The flying carries on through most showers and the sun often comes out for the afternoon.

I would take a light waterproof that packs into a pocket, and keep a dry layer in a bag for when the shower has passed and the wind picks up. Use the showers as your break. Food and toilet queues are shorter when everyone else is hiding.

  • Packable waterproof and a warm layer
  • A bin bag to sit your chairs and bag on
  • Sun cream as well. Showery days burn people
My tip: some of the best photos come right after a shower, with dark cloud behind and sun on the aircraft.
Heavy rain

Go prepared

The show will most likely still open, and some flying usually still happens. It just won't be as comfortable, so this is where packing well pays off.

I would wear proper waterproofs top and bottom, with wellies or walking boots, and leave the jeans at home as they take forever to dry. Plan time indoors in the hangars or under cover, and check the morning updates for any changes to parking.

  • Waterproof trousers and wellies
  • Spare socks for everyone, kept in a sealed bag
  • Towel and a change of clothes left in the car
  • Rain cover for your camera and the pushchair
My tip: pay attention to updates about the car parks the night before and on the morning. Grass car parks are the first thing heavy rain affects.
Low cloud

Expect a flat show

Low cloud is the forecast that actually changes the flying. The teams switch to rolling or flat displays, and some of the fast jets may not fly at all if the cloud sits very low or the visibility is poor.

Warbirds and helicopters usually carry on as they fly lower anyway. I would still go. The aircraft stay close to the crowd line and the sound is huge. Cloud often lifts during the day too, so the afternoon can look quite different.

  • A warm layer. Grey days feel colder on an open airfield
  • Camera settings for low light, see my photography hub
  • Patience, and a plan to see the static show while you wait
My tip: run your forecast through the slider on the Weather and will it fly guide to see which aircraft are likely to fly.
Wind

Wrap up warm

Strong wind can stop some displays, especially the older and lighter aircraft, and gusts across the runway can make take offs tricky. The big jets usually cope fine.

On the crowd line it is the cold that gets you. An open airfield has nothing to stop the wind, so I would dress for a temperature a few degrees lower than the forecast. Windbreaks need pegging properly, and if you have a small pop up shelter, think about whether it is worth putting up at all.

  • Hat that won't blow off, gloves if it's early or late in the season
  • Pegs and a mallet for the windbreak
  • Keep light bags and blankets weighed down
My tip: umbrellas are hard work in wind and block the view for people behind, so I leave mine at home and rely on a good hood.

I'll be honest with you here, as this one catches people out. If the show opens its gates and some flying happens, you are very unlikely to get money back, even if your favourite display was scrubbed.

Almost every show I have looked at says the flying programme depends on the weather and the serviceability of the aircraft, and that a shorter or changed programme is not a cancellation. When I last checked, the terms at Duxford and Shuttleworth both said there were no refunds even if an event was cancelled, with Shuttleworth only refunding if you had bought its ticket protection. RIAT talks about refunds or a rescheduled event only if the whole thing is cancelled or postponed.

Free seafront shows are a little different, as there is no ticket to refund. Paid grandstand seats or parking passes at those shows will have their own terms though.

  1. Read the terms before you buy

    Look for the words cancellation, weather and refunds. It takes two minutes and you'll know where you stand.

  2. Think about ticket protection or insurance

    Some shows offer it at checkout. It may work for you if you are travelling a long way or booking a hotel.

  3. Book flexible accommodation if you can

    The ticket is often the cheapest part. A free cancellation room gives you more options if the show itself is called off.

  4. Keep your booking emails

    If a show is cancelled, the organisers usually email ticket holders with what happens next.

Please see the rules before you go. Every show writes its own terms and they change from year to year, so check the official page for your show rather than going on what happened last time.

Your full packing list is in the What to pack guide. These are the extras I add when rain is on the way.

Layers are everything. An airfield is flat and open, and once you are wet and the wind gets up you cool down fast, even in July. I would wear a warm layer under a proper waterproof, then add or take off as the day goes on.

Feet matter most. Wet trainers will ruin the day by lunchtime. Wellies or waterproof walking boots are the way to go, and a pair of dry socks in a sandwich bag feels like luxury at three in the afternoon.

Coats on, chairs out. A typical British crowd line.

  • Waterproof jacket with a hoodA hood beats an umbrella on a crowd line.
  • Waterproof trousersCheap overtrousers are fine for one day.
  • Wellies or waterproof bootsGrass gets muddy around the busy areas fast.
  • Spare socks and a dry topKept in a sealed bag inside your rucksack.
  • Bin bagsTo sit on, and for the wet gear on the way home.
  • Waterproof cover for your rucksackOr a bin bag liner inside it.
  • A flask of something hotSoup or tea. It makes a cold afternoon much better.
  • A change of clothes in the carFor the drive home, you will thank yourself.
I would advise checking the show's rules on gazebos and large umbrellas before you pack them. Many shows don't allow them on the crowd line because they block the view for everyone behind.

Children cope with rain better than adults think. They don't cope with being cold, wet and bored all at once. If you can sort out the first two, the third is much easier.

An all in one puddle suit is the best thing I have seen parents bring. It goes over their normal clothes, they can sit on wet grass and nobody minds. Wellies, a hat and spare gloves go a long way too.

Keeping them dry

  • Puddle suit or waterproof trousers and coat
  • A rain cover for the pushchair, and a blanket for underneath it
  • Two full sets of spare clothes in the car
  • A camping mat or bin bag to sit on

Keeping them busy

  • Plan a walk round the static aircraft between showers
  • A pair of cheap binoculars each
  • Spotter sheets or a list of aircraft to tick off
  • Card games or a tablet for the slow bits
  • Treats saved for when the display team arrives

Children love to see inside the aircraft, and on a wet day this is a good way to get them under a roof. Some aircraft on the static display are opened up for you to take a look inside, and you can often buy exclusive merch in or outside the aircraft, which goes down well with young fans.

Ear defenders still matter on a grey day. A flat show keeps the jets low and close, so it can be even louder. Your child's ears are delicate, so try to protect them as much as possible.

This might work for your family: sit near the end of the crowd line closest to a hangar or covered area, so you can nip inside with the little ones without losing your spot for long.

The static aircraft are still there whatever the weather.

The shows held at museums are the best places to be on a wet day, as you get a whole collection under cover with your ticket. Seafront shows and the big military bases have far less, so it's worth knowing what you're heading into.

At most shows you'll find covered trade marquees, the food areas and sometimes a display team or forces village with tents. They fill up fast when it pours, so I would go in at the start of a shower, not the middle of it.

Museum show

IWM Duxford

When I last checked, the hangars and exhibitions stay open all day during the air shows. Several huge buildings full of aircraft, plus cafes and the shop. Easily a couple of hours if the weather closes in.

Museum show

RAF Cosford

The show is held at the RAF Museum site, which has several hangars including the National Cold War Exhibition and Test Flight. On show day the museum is open to air show ticket holders, so check which buildings are open before you go.

Museum show

Shuttleworth

The collection's hangars are full of vintage aircraft and cars, and some of what you see inside will be flying later. Some hangars are working areas and closed to visitors, so follow the signs.

At a seafront show? The town is your shelter. Cafes, arcades and pubs along the front are all options, and you can still hear the jets from inside. Food and drink at airshows has more on eating at the shows.

I've shot plenty of airshows in the rain and the camera has always survived. A few cheap bits of kit make the difference.

Weather sealing on a camera body helps, but the lens mount and some lenses are often the weak points. I would use a proper rain cover, and if you don't have one, a clear plastic bag with a hole for the lens and an elastic band does the job for a day.

Keep the lens hood on. It stops most drops landing on the front element. Carry a couple of microfibre cloths in a sealed bag, as a damp cloth just smears the water around.

Condensation catches people out at the end of the day. Moving a cold, wet camera into a warm car fogs everything up. I leave mine in the closed camera bag for a while to warm up slowly, and put a few silica gel sachets in the bag.

Damp air gives you vapour. The pay off for getting wet.

Worth taking

  • Rain cover or a clear bag and elastic band
  • Two or three microfibre cloths
  • Silica gel sachets in the camera bag
  • Spare batteries, as cold drains them faster

Worth avoiding

  • Changing lenses in the open during a shower
  • Wiping the lens with your sleeve
  • Opening the bag straight away in a warm car

What a few hours of heavy rain does to a busy grass field.

Most airshow car parks are grass fields. After a lot of rain, thousands of cars turn the gateways into a mess, and the way out at the end of the day is always worse than the way in.

If heavy rain has fallen in the days before, keep an eye on the show's social pages. Organisers sometimes close a field, move parking or push people towards park and ride. The public transport guide is worth a look as a plan B.

  1. Park where the marshals tell you

    They know which parts of the field drain and which don't. I see others pick their own spot and regret it later.

  2. Leave a gap and point your wheels straight

    Pulling straight out is far easier than turning on wet grass.

  3. Drive out gently

    Low gear, light on the accelerator, no sudden steering. In a manual, try pulling away in second. Spinning the wheels just digs you in deeper.

  4. If you get stuck, stop and ask

    Many shows have tractors or 4x4s on hand to tow cars out. Flag down a marshal rather than spinning the wheels.

  5. Bring something for the boot

    An old towel, a bin bag for muddy boots and a pair of shoes to drive home in.

I would advise staying a little longer after the finale on a wet day. Once the first rush has churned up the exit and gone, it is calmer, and the tow crews have time for you if you need them.

I've camped near RAF Fairford for years and had a few properly wet ones. The Camping at airshows guide covers the full picture, so here are just the rain bits.

Pitch on the highest flat bit of your space, never in a dip, as that's where the water collects overnight. Keep your groundsheet tucked inside the edge of the tent so rain can't run down the walls and under you.

Make a wet area and a dry area. Wet coats and boots stay in the porch or a gazebo, and nothing wet goes into the sleeping area. It sounds fussy. On day three you'll be glad you did it.

If wind comes with the rain, peg out every guy line and take gazebo canopies down if strong gusts are forecast.

When the sky looks like this, check your guy lines.

Dry bags

Pack clothes and sleeping bags in dry bags or bin bags inside your holdall.

Spare pegs

Wet ground lets go of pegs easily. Longer ones hold better.

Towels

One for people and an old one just for wiping the tent and the chairs.

Power bank

You'll check the forecast and the show updates more than usual.

Think about packing the tent away wet if you have to, then drying it out at home within a day or two. A tent left damp in its bag will go mouldy.

Our view

Some of my best airshow memories are from grey, damp days. The crowds are thinner and the jets stay low and loud against a dramatic sky. Pack well, keep your plans loose and go anyway.
