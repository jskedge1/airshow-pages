Weather and will it fly?

What really happens when the cloud comes in, and why you should still go

The weather decides more about an airshow than anything else, but not in the way most people expect. Shows go ahead in the rain. What changes is the shape of the flying.

Rain does not cancel a show, cloud shapes it

What to expect when the weather is poor

Here is the thing most first time visitors do not know. Airshows are very rarely cancelled for rain. The gates open, the static show is there, the stalls are open and the flying goes ahead as far as the weather allows.

What changes is the shape of the flying. Every pilot and team has limits for how high the cloud is and how far they can see. With plenty of clear sky above you get the full show, with loops and vertical climbs. Lower down you get a rolling show, with everything kept under the cloud. Lower still you get a flat show of passes and formation changes. Below that, the jets stay on the ground and the organisers shuffle the programme around.

So a grey day usually still has flying, just lower and closer. Some people actually prefer it, as the aircraft spend more time in front of you and less time at the top of a loop.

Low cloud does not stop the flying. It brings it down to where you can see it.

Full showCloud above 5,500 ft

Loops, vertical breaks and the big formation shapes. This is the day everyone hopes for.

The loops come out and wingovers and rolls go in. Still a full length display, just a lower one.

In plain terms. The heights on these cards are the ones the Red Arrows use, and most display teams work in a similar way. Pack for rain, come anyway and you will almost always see flying.

What photographers can expect

Each shape of show puts the aircraft in a different part of the sky, and that changes your lens, your shutter speed and where you stand. The box that matches your reading is marked in teal.

I would suggest checking the show's official Facebook page or X account from about 09:00 on the day. The Flying Display Director makes the call with each pilot, using the weather at height over the airfield, and it can change by the hour. The numbers here are a guide to help you plan, not the limits for any one show on the day.

Cloud base here means the cloud ceiling: the height above the airfield of the lowest cloud covering more than half of the sky. Full, rolling and flat are the three shapes of a fast jet or formation display. A full show has loops and vertical manoeuvres, a rolling show keeps everything below the cloud with rolls and wingovers, and a flat show is passes and formation changes at low level. The legal minimum for any formation display is a 1,000 ft cloud ceiling with 5 km visibility, and a full aerobatic jet formation needs at least 1,500 ft before the team's own higher limits come in.

The limits the pilots work to

What the rules say, and what the teams add on top

Every flying display in the UK runs under rules set by the Civil Aviation Authority, and military teams such as the Red Arrows have their own rules on top. There are minimum weather conditions that nobody can fly below, and each team and pilot then sets higher limits of their own.

The number that matters most is the cloud ceiling. That is the height above the airfield of the lowest cloud that covers more than half of the sky. A few scattered clouds at 1,500 ft on an otherwise blue day do not count, so please do not panic if you see some low puffs on the drive in.

The person who makes the call on the day is the Flying Display Director, working with each pilot. I would advise you not to write a display off just because it looks grey from the car park, as the decision is made on the weather at height and it can change by the hour.

Why the airfield matters

Some sites make certain aircraft harder to display

Every airfield has its own quirks and they affect what you see. Runway direction, the surface, the length and what is around the site all play a part.

A small grass airfield such as Old Warden or Little Gransden can only take lighter aircraft, so the big jets and transports fly their displays in from bases elsewhere. RAF Fairford has one of the longest runways in the country, which is why the heavy transports and bombers can land and sit on the static park at the Air Tattoo.

Wind is the big one. Aircraft take off and land into wind, so if the wind is blowing across the runway, called a crosswind, every landing is harder. Modern jets have wide limits. Vintage tail draggers with narrow undercarriages have very small ones, so a Spitfire or a Hurricane may be grounded by a crosswind that a Typhoon would not notice.

Seafront shows have no runway at all. The aircraft fly in from an airfield nearby, so the weather there matters as much as the weather on the beach. Sea fog is the classic problem, and in hazy weather the sea and sky can merge into one, which pilots call the goldfish bowl effect. The town can be in sunshine while the display line out over the water is not safe to fly.

Grass airfields

Shows built around warbirds

The best flying on a good day, the most fragile on a bad one

Historic aircraft are the first to be affected by cloud, crosswind and heavy rain.

Duxford, Shuttleworth and the other historic shows are built around warbirds and vintage aircraft. On a fine day there is nothing like it. On a poor day these are the shows most likely to lose items from the programme, because the aircraft are old, light and irreplaceable, and their pilots fly to tighter limits.

Crosswinds are the main problem for landing, and many warbirds have a crosswind limit somewhere around 10 to 15 knots. Heavy rain is hard on fabric and wooden aircraft and on open cockpits. Low cloud takes away the height a loop needs. The organisers will juggle the programme, fly what can fly and hold back what cannot, so you will usually still see a show, just not quite the one in the programme.

Jets, helicopters and modern military aircraft are far more tolerant. Helicopters have the lowest weather limits of anything at a show, so a Chinook will often display in weather that keeps everything else on the ground.

I would suggest that if you are travelling a long way for a particular warbird, you look at the forecast wind and cloud base a few days out, and keep a second show in mind. If you are going for the day out, go anyway. The ground show and the modern aircraft will carry it.

The positives of poor weather

Why some of my favourite photographs came on grey days

Grey days have an upside. A rolling or flat show keeps the aircraft lower and closer, so you see more of them and hear more of them. A damp, heavy sky pulls vapour off the wings of a fast jet in a turn, which is one of the best sights at any show. Soft light has no harsh shadows, and a dark cloud behind a bright aircraft makes it stand out.

If you are bringing a camera, the photography hub has a weather section on settings for dull light, and the sun guide on each show page shows where the light will be if the cloud breaks.

Viewing

Lower and closer

Flat and rolling shows put the aircraft in front of you at low level for longer. Great for seeing detail.

Photography

Vapour and condensation

Humid air and hard turns give you vapour over the wings and sometimes a full cone. Fast jets only.

Bonus

Thinner crowds

Rain keeps some people at home. Easier parking, shorter queues and more room on the crowd line.

Our View

Check the forecast, then come anyway. Bring a waterproof and a sun hat in the same bag, because you will probably need both. The flying adapts to the sky and the ground show carries on regardless. The only day I would think twice about is a long trip for one particular vintage aircraft in a strong crosswind.
