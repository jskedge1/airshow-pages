A Calm Airshow Visit

For anyone who finds the noise and the crowds hard

Airshows are loud and they get busy, and for some of us that is a lot. With good ear protection and a plan for when it gets too much, a calm day out is very possible.

This guide is here to help you plan a calmer day for anyone who finds the noise and the crowds hard. That might be an autistic child or adult, someone who gets anxious in busy places, a baby at their very first show, or a grandparent who would just like a bit of peace. Everyone is different. Take what works for you and leave the rest.

The show you pick makes the biggest difference. The big military shows bring the fast jets and the biggest crowds. Smaller shows are usually gentler, with more room to spread out and fewer sudden bangs.

If a show runs over more than one day, I have found the first day is often a little less busy, although that is not a rule. Once the flying programme comes out, have a look at when the fast jets are on. You can plan your breaks around them.

Unfortunately the weather on the day will be what it will be, so plan accordingly. If it's fresh, dress in layers to keep warm. A hot, packed day can make everything feel louder, so shade and water really do matter.

Vintage aircraft growl rather than roar. A gentler place to start.

Gentler

Vintage And Grass Airfield Shows

Shuttleworth and Headcorn lean on older aircraft, so the sound is more of a deep growl. Fast jets are rare at Shuttleworth. I would still check the line up before you book, as it changes every year.

Smaller crowds

Small Country Shows

Little Gransden and the smaller airfield shows feel more relaxed. There is room to sit back from the fence, and walking out to the car takes minutes, not an hour.

Room to move

Seafront Shows

Shows like Eastbourne, Clacton and Southport stretch along miles of seafront with no gates. You can walk away from the busy middle whenever you like. Fast jets do still appear, so bring ear protection.

Loudest

Big Military Shows

RIAT, Cosford and the big Duxford days are the loudest and busiest of the lot. They are still doable with a plan, and RIAT and Duxford both list a quiet space.

I would suggest watching a few videos of the aircraft at home before you go. Knowing what a Typhoon or a Spitfire sounds like takes away a lot of the surprise, and children love to see the aircraft they have been watching on screen turn up for real.

Towards the end of the line, the crowd thins out and there is room to breathe.

Most people gather in the middle of the crowd line, opposite the commentary position. That is also where the speakers are loudest and the rows of chairs are deepest.

Walk towards either end and it thins out quickly. You lose a little of the action, but the aircraft are big and they cover the whole display line. You will still see plenty.

I would advise picking a spot with an easy way out behind you. Being boxed in by chairs and windbreaks can feel worse than the noise itself for some people. Sitting a few rows back from the fence, near a walkway, means you can get up and go without asking anyone to move.

At seafront shows the higher ground is calmer. At Clacton, the high ground at Holland on Sea is a quieter place to watch, further from the crowds. There is more on choosing a spot in the where to watch guide.

Ends Of The Line

Fewer people, less commentary noise, and you are often closer to an exit.

A Few Rows Back

Space around you and a clear path out. The view is still good.

Away From The Speakers

Look for the tall speaker poles along the line and sit between them, not under them.

Away From Food Courts

Generators, music and queues all add to the noise. Visit them, then move away.

A base you control. A car or a tent can be your own quiet space. Some shows have park and view areas where you watch from beside the car, and camping gives you somewhere to retreat to all weekend. The camping guide has more on that.

Fast jets are loud enough to hurt, even for people who love the noise. Your child's ears are delicate, so try to protect them as much as possible. The same goes for grown ups too, if we are honest.

Pick the age below to see what I would suggest.

Look for ear defenders made for babies, with a soft band that adjusts to a small head. They are light and most babies seem to forget they are wearing them.

Never put foam earplugs in a small child's ears. They can be a choking risk.

Put them on before the flying starts, not when the first jet arrives. A baby asleep in the pram may well stay asleep, and a sunshade over the top helps block out some of the busyness too.

Worth Packing

  • Baby ear defenders, plus a spare
  • A pram sunshade or light cover
  • Their usual comforter
  • A quiet place in mind for feeds

Children's ear defenders are cheap and easy to find. Let them wear them at home for a few days first, while they watch television or play, so they feel normal by the day of the show.

Letting them pick a bright colour or add a few stickers can help a lot. It turns them into their own thing.

I would suggest keeping them on for the whole flying display, jets or no jets. Taking them off and on again is where the surprises happen.

Worth Packing

  • Over ear defenders in a size for children
  • A spare pair
  • A name label inside the band
  • Something to hold or fiddle with

Foam earplugs work well and fit in a pocket. Reusable filtered earplugs turn the volume down while keeping the sound clear, which suits anyone who still wants to enjoy the roar.

If you cannot stand anything in your ear, over ear defenders are the answer. Check the SNR number on the packet, which tells you how much noise they cut. The higher the number, the more they cut.

Noise cancelling headphones are great for the steady drone of a crowd and the generators. For the fast jets, I would still add earplugs or ear defenders.

Worth Packing

  • Earplugs in a small case
  • Ear defenders if earplugs feel wrong
  • Headphones with music or a podcast
  • Sunglasses, for bright glare off the sky

If you wear hearing aids, I would suggest asking your audiologist before you go about what to do at loud events. They will know your aids and your hearing.

If you have tinnitus, protect your ears from the start of the flying, rather than waiting for the loud ones to arrive.

A long day on your feet is tiring in itself, and tired makes everything harder. A chair with a back, some shade and a spot near the toilets can make the day much more comfortable.

Worth Packing

  • Ear defenders or earplugs
  • A folding chair with a back
  • Any medication, with a little extra
  • Spare hearing aid batteries
Pack a spare pair. They get dropped, sat on, and lent to the family next to you whose child forgot theirs. The what to pack guide has the full list.

Not every display is loud. A biplane drifting round the sky is gentle. A Typhoon on full afterburner is something else, and you feel it in your chest as much as hear it.

The RAF Typhoon is the one I would plan around. The pilot lights the afterburners on take off and in the tight turns, and that is when the sound hits hardest. It comes in waves through the whole display, so it is not one bang and done.

Other fast jets from abroad, like the F‑16, Rafale and Gripen, are much the same. The F‑35 is very loud too, especially when it slows right down and hangs in the air.

A Typhoon with the afterburners lit. For most people this is the loudest moment of the day.

Roughly How Loud

Fast jets on afterburnerTyphoon, F‑35, F‑16, Rafale, Gripen. The loudest of the day. A sudden, very deep roar.
Military helicoptersChinook and Apache. A heavy thudding you feel as well as hear. Loud close up.
Jet display teamsThe Red Arrows. Loud on the close passes and the crossovers, calmer in between.
Big transport aircraft and bombersA long, deep rumble. Loud, but it builds slowly so it rarely makes you jump.
Spitfires and other warbirdsA loud growl, but a rounder sound with fewer surprises.
Biplanes, gliders and light aircraftThe quiet end of the day. Some aerobatic aircraft buzz, but they rarely startle.

Warning Signs Before A Loud One

  1. Listen To The Commentary

    The commentator names each display before it starts. If it is a fast jet, that is your moment to put ear defenders on.

  2. Know The Programme

    When the flying times come out, mark the fast jets. Some shows put the times on their app or social pages on the day as well.

  3. Watch For A Take Off

    At an airfield show the jet usually waits at the end of the runway first. A rising roar from that direction means it is about to go.

  4. You See It Before You Hear It

    Sound travels slower than a fast jet. If you see one coming in low and quick, the noise will land a moment later, so cover ears straight away.

  5. Watch The Photographers

    When the long lenses along the fence all lift at once, something fast is on its way.

Seafront shows have no take off, so the jets arrive from a distance with less warning. The commentary is your best clue there.

A few shows have offered a quiet space or extra support on the day. These are the ones I could confirm when I last checked. Arrangements change from year to year, so please check with the show before you go.

RIAT

The Wellness Room

If you need a quiet place to take a break and recharge, ask at the Central Information Point and a team member will direct you to the Wellness Room. Bear in mind nowhere on an airfield is fully quiet once the flying starts.

RIAT show page

IWM Duxford

Quiet Room And Sensory Backpacks

Duxford has a Quiet Room behind the 19 Squadron Restaurant, open to all visitors and meant for people with sensory needs among others. You can borrow a sensory backpack for free from the Visitor Centre or the Visit Planning Point in Hangar 1, with ear defenders and a sunflower lanyard inside. I would ask on the day how both work during an air show, as the site is far busier.

Duxford show page

Wales National Airshow

National Autistic Society Support

In Swansea, supporters from the National Autistic Society Swansea branch have helped families at the accessible viewing areas by the Cenotaph and the Civic Centre. The Civic Centre area is close to Swansea Library inside the building, which is somewhere quieter if the noise gets too much.

Ayr

A Quiet Space In The Welfare Marquee

In 2026 TLC Welfare had a quiet space in their marquee, on your right as you come in from Wellington Square. Worth knowing about if someone needs a break from the noise.

Ayr show page

At Eastbourne in 2023 and 2024 there was an accessible viewing area for families with profound and multiple learning disabilities, with sunshades and beds. I have not seen it confirmed since, so I would ask the council before you go.

If your show is not listed, ask anyway. Email or ring a few weeks ahead and explain what would help. Organisers are often happy to point you to a calmer corner, even if it is not on the website.

On the day, the first aid tent and the information point are good places to head if someone is overwhelmed. The staff there are used to helping, and it is usually calmer inside.

The Hidden Disabilities Sunflower is a green lanyard with yellow sunflowers on it. Wearing one tells staff that you may need a bit more time, patience or help, without you having to explain. It started at Gatwick in 2016, and you will now see it at airports, stations and lots of venues.

You do not need a diagnosis or any proof to wear one. It is your choice whether you share it.

It is not a ticket or a pass, though. On its own it will not get you into a viewing area or past a queue. What it can do is help a steward or a marshal understand when you need to step out of a line, sit down or leave in a hurry.

I have not seen many airshows say their staff are trained in the scheme, so I would still tell people what you need. Duxford puts one in its sensory backpacks, which is a nice touch.

Getting One

Many places that take part in the scheme give them out free. You can also buy one from the official Sunflower website.

I would advise wearing it from the moment you join the gate queue. That is often the busiest, most crowded part of the day, and the security guide explains what happens at the gate so there are no surprises.

Gate queues build quickly. The first hour and the early afternoon tend to be calmer.

Ask yourself which is harder, the crowds or the noise. That usually decides it.

The first hour after the gates open is usually the calmest time of the day. If you want a spot at the end of the crowd line, head there first and save your pitch. Then look around the static show while it is still quiet, speak to the pilots, visit the trade stalls and check the food options. Some aircraft are opened up for you to take a look inside, and you can buy exclusive merch in or outside the aircraft.

Visit the toilet before the flying display starts. If you are not waiting to see a particular display, use it as a toilet break.

Arriving after lunch suits some families. The gate queues have gone and the day is shorter. You will still catch the big afternoon displays, so bring ear protection all the same.

Arriving Early

  • Quiet gates and car parks
  • First pick of a calmer spot
  • The static show and toilets without queues
  • A long day, so plan rest breaks

Arriving Late

  • A shorter day with fewer loud displays
  • No gate queue
  • Less choice of spot, so expect to sit further back
  • Check the show still lets people in later
Check whether you can come back in. Some shows let you pop back to the car and return, and some do not. A short break at the car can reset the whole day, so it is worth knowing before you go. Each show page has this in its info boxes.

I would plan the way out before anything else. It sounds odd. Knowing exactly what happens if it all gets too much takes a lot of the worry out of the day, for the person who struggles and for everyone with them.

  1. Agree A Signal

    A word, a card or a hand sign that means "I need a break now". No questions asked when it is used.

  2. Pick A Meeting Point

    A large static aircraft or a numbered sign. Show it to your child when you arrive and take a photo of it on your phone.

  3. Know Where Help Is

    Find the first aid tent and the information point early. Staff there are used to helping someone who is overwhelmed or a child who has lost their family.

  4. Have A Quiet Place To Go

    The car, the tent, a quiet room if the show has one, or simply the far end of the site.

  5. Decide When You Will Leave

    Before the finale, or well after it. The rush straight after the last display is the busiest moment of the whole day.

Leaving early is completely fine. I would much rather someone went home at 15:00 having had a good day than stayed to the end and found it all too much.

If you can, park near the exit of the car park. Packing up during a display you are not bothered about means you are walking out before the crowd moves. The on the day guide covers which shows are known for long exit queues.

I would advise writing a name and a phone number on a wristband or a card in your child's pocket. If they get separated and find it hard to talk to a stranger, it does the talking for them.

My view

The noise is part of an airshow, but it does not have to be the whole day. I would rather see a family come for three hours with ear defenders and a way out than stay at home. If you are unsure, start small with a vintage show and work up to the big jets another year.
