Getting Home From An Airshow

Car parks, last trains, taxis and tired children at the end of a long day

Everybody plans the drive there. Hardly anyone plans the trip home, and that is when people get stuck. Decide before the finale how and when you are leaving, and the last hour of the day stays a good one.

There are really only two good times to leave an airshow. Before the last display, or a good while after it. The worst time is the ten minutes after the finale, when thousands of people fold their chairs at once and head for the same gates.

Leaving early works well when you have young children who are flagging, a long drive ahead or a train you cannot miss. Pack up during the second to last display, watch the last one standing near the exit, then walk out. You miss a little of the flying. You also miss most of the queue.

Staying late is what I do at nearly every show. The static aircraft are still there, the trade stalls are quieter and some shows have aircraft taxiing back in or departing after the flying. Some of the best photographs of the day come in that last hour. By the time I walk back, the car park is half empty.

If you do stay for departures, keep the ear defenders handy. Your child's ears are delicate, so try to protect them as much as possible, and a jet spooling up on the taxiway is loud.

The quiet hour after the flying. Aircraft parked up and room to walk around.

Leave early if

  • You have a train or bus you cannot afford to miss
  • The children are tired and the mood is turning
  • You face a long drive and started at dawn
  • You have seen what you came for

Stay late if

  • You came by car and hate sitting in queues
  • You enjoy the static show and photography
  • The show has departures after the flying
  • You are camping nearby or walking home
I would suggest deciding which one you are doing before the finale starts. The people who get stuck in the worst of it are usually the ones who could not make their minds up and left at exactly the wrong moment.

Grass car parks empty a row at a time. Be patient, the marshals will get to you.

Most airshow car parks are fields. They fill in the morning over three or four hours, and then everyone tries to leave in about thirty minutes. The field exits feed onto country lanes, the lanes feed onto one or two main roads, and the police or marshals often hold traffic so the roads stay safe.

So it goes in waves. The rows nearest the exit move first. The rest of the field sits still with engines running, then suddenly moves.

In my experience you can expect anything from 30 minutes to an hour and a half to get out of a big show car park if you leave with the crowd. Some shows are worse. Duxford has a long wait because so much traffic goes out onto one junction, and the seafront shows are slow because the town roads reopen and everybody heads for the same few routes. I cover the slowest ones on the On the day guide.

  1. Photograph where you parked

    Take a picture of the row marker or a landmark in the morning. Fields look identical at 18:00, I promise you.

  2. Park facing the way out if you can

    Follow the marshals, but if you get a choice, reversing in makes leaving quicker and a lot less stressful.

  3. Keep drinks and snacks in the car

    Something for the queue makes a huge difference, especially with children. A full bottle of water each is my minimum.

  4. Turn the engine off if you are not moving

    When the field is standing still, there is no point burning fuel. Wind the windows down and wait for the row in front to go.

  5. Check the roads before you set off

    A quick look at a traffic map tells you whether the motorway is clear or whether a different route home would be better.

This is the bit I feel most strongly about. Sunday evening services are often thinner than people expect, and engineering work is usually booked for weekends. The last train home can be a lot earlier than you think, or it can be a rail replacement bus.

Check your last train or bus the night before, and again on the morning of the show. Then screenshot it. Signal at a big show is poor once thousands of phones are in one field, and you do not want to be refreshing a journey planner that will not load while the queue for the station builds.

Then work backwards. How long is the walk or shuttle to the station? Add twenty minutes for the crowds. That is the time you need to be leaving the crowd line, and it is often before the finale.

  1. Screenshot the last two departures

    The last one and the one before it. If the last one is cancelled, you want to know what your options were.

  2. Check for engineering works

    Look at the whole journey home, not just the first train. A replacement bus on the second leg can add an hour.

  3. Set an alarm on your phone

    When the flying is good, time goes quickly. An alarm for "start walking now" saves a lot of running.

  4. Buy your ticket before you leave the showground

    Ticket machines at small stations get a queue after a show. Use an app or buy a return in the morning.

Bus passes

In England, the older person's bus pass is free on local buses from 09:30 to 23:00 on weekdays and all day at weekends and on bank holidays, so a Saturday or Sunday show is fine. Scotland and Wales have their own passes, so please check yours before you go.

Shuttle buses and Park and Ride are brilliant in the morning. You skip the traffic, get dropped near the gate and walk straight in. In the evening, they have a queue, because every bus fills with the same crowd that just watched the finale.

The buses usually keep running for a while after the flying ends, and the queue goes down steadily. When I last checked, RIAT ran Park and Ride sites plus a shuttle bus from Swindon, and plenty of the smaller shows run a minibus from the nearest station. Times change every year, so check the show's own website before you go.

If you want a short queue, go before the finale or wait an hour after it. If you are happy to queue, bring a drink, keep a layer on and enjoy the chat. Unfortunately the weather on the day will be what it will be, so plan accordingly. If it's fresh, dress in layers to keep warm, as standing in a queue at 18:00 can feel a lot colder than sitting in the sun at 14:00.

Queues like this clear faster than they look. Give it twenty minutes.

I would advise asking the driver in the morning what time the last bus back leaves, and where it goes from. The pickup point at the end of the day is not always where you were dropped off.

Local taxi firms near an airshow are small. They might have six cars, and on show day every one of them is booked. Waving at a passing cab will not work in a country lane, and ride hailing apps often show nothing at all in rural areas.

Book your taxi home before the day. Ring the firm a week or two ahead, give them the show name and agree a time and a meeting place. Then ring again on the morning to confirm. If you need a wheelchair accessible car, book even earlier, because most firms only have one or two.

Agree a pickup point away from the main gate, such as a pub, a village hall or a named lay by. The roads by the gate are often closed to cars or jammed solid. Walking ten minutes to a quieter spot will save you far more than ten minutes.

Book

A week or two ahead for a big show. Give the firm the show name, your pickup point and a mobile number.

Confirm

Ring on the morning of the show. Plans change, and a quick call makes sure your car is still on the sheet.

Be flexible

Ask for a time an hour after the finale. The driver gets through the traffic easier, and you get a quieter walk out.

At the end of the day everyone stands up at once. It is easy to lose each other.

Groups tend to split on the way out. Someone goes for a last toilet trip, someone else wants one more look at the stalls, and suddenly you are four people in four places with no signal.

Agree a meeting point for going home as well as one for the day. Make it something fixed and easy to find near your exit, like a named gate, a numbered car park sign or a big static aircraft that is not moving. Agree a time too. "The blue gate at 18:15" works far better than "see you by the car".

Children should know the plan as well. I would suggest writing your mobile number on their arm or a wristband, and telling them to find a steward or a first aid tent if they get lost. Stewards deal with this every show and they are very good at it.

Phone battery

Your phone is your ticket, your map, your train times and your torch. Photos, videos and searching for a signal all drain it fast. I keep at least 30% for the trip home, turn the brightness down after lunch and pack a charged power bank. The What to pack guide has more on that.

If the signal goes

Texts often get through when calls and data will not. Send a short text with where you are, then stay put at the meeting point rather than walking about looking for each other.

Children love to see the Red Arrows and the big jets. They do not love the walk back to a field a mile away at the end of a hot day. That walk is often when the tears start, so I would plan for it the same way you plan for the morning.

A folding trolley or a pushchair is worth its weight on the way out. It carries the chairs in the morning and a sleepy child in the evening. Keep one snack hidden for the walk back. Honestly, it works.

If your little ones are flagging by mid afternoon, this might work for your family: leave before the finale, let them watch the last display from near the exit, and be on the road before the rush. You have still had a great day, and nobody has a meltdown in a car park queue.

  1. Change them into something comfy for the drive

    A clean top and a pair of soft trousers in the car turns a long drive into a nap.

  2. Toilet before you leave the showground

    The car park queue is no place to find out someone needs to go. Make it the last stop on the way out.

  3. Something for the queue

    A tablet, a book or a game kept back for the journey. Children who have been looking up at the sky all day are happy to look down at a screen for a bit.

This is the bit people underestimate. You were up at five, you have been on your feet in the sun or the wind for ten hours, and now you have two or three hours of motorway in front of you. That is a lot.

The Highway Code recommends a break of at least 15 minutes after every two hours of driving. If you feel sleepy, stop somewhere safe. Never on the hard shoulder of a motorway. A caffeine drink and a short nap of about 15 minutes help, but they only buy you a little time.

If you can, share the driving, or let someone who did not drive there take the wheel. And keep drinking water through the afternoon, because being dried out makes tiredness a lot worse.

It has been a long day. Be honest with yourself about how tired you are.

Signs to pull over. Yawning a lot, heavy eyes, drifting in the lane or not remembering the last few miles. If you notice any of those, find the next services or a safe place to stop. Do not push on to get home.
How about this. If you live a long way from the show, book a room nearby for the night and drive home in the morning. Camping is another option and you skip the car park altogether. The camping guide covers that.

It happens to plenty of people, so please do not panic. What you do next depends on why you missed it.

If an earlier train was late or cancelled and that made you miss your last connection, speak to the station staff or ring the train company. The train companies are expected to get stranded passengers to their destination, sometimes with a bus or a taxi, or find them somewhere to stay. Keep your ticket and any receipts.

If you simply missed it, because the queue was long or the finale ran late, it is down to you to get home. That is when a bit of planning in the morning pays off.

  1. Check for a later route

    Sometimes a different station or a bus to a bigger town gets you onto a later train. Station staff know these well, so ask.

  2. Ring a local taxi firm

    Have two or three numbers saved before the day. A taxi to a bigger station with later trains can cost far less than a taxi all the way home.

  3. Look for a room nearby

    On a show weekend rooms go quickly, but late cancellations do come up. A night in a local pub or a budget hotel is better than a two hour taxi.

  4. Let someone know

    Text family with your plan and how much battery you have left. Then keep your phone for calls.

I would recommend saving the number of a local taxi firm and the nearest hotel in your phone before you leave home. You will probably never use them. If you do, you will be very glad you did.

Our view

Sitting in a field with the engine off for an hour is nobody's idea of fun, and neither is running for a train with a camera bag. Make the call before the finale and stick to it. For me that usually means staying an hour, wandering the static line and driving out of a half empty field. With tired children or a tight train, going early is just as good a choice.
