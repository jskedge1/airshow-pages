How much kit do you really need?

The biggest mistake I see at airshows is people carrying far more than they use. By lunchtime the bag is heavy, the second lens has not left it, and the best pass of the day goes by while someone is swapping kit. Pick the setup below that sounds most like you. I would suggest starting light, then adding only what you know you will use.

On the day

5 tips
  • If you want a front row seat, head there first and save your pitch before you do anything else.
  • Then look around the static show, speak to the pilots, visit the trade stalls and check the food options. This is the best time for wide lenses and close detail shots.
  • Visit the toilet before the flying display starts. If you are not waiting for a particular display, use it as a toilet break and a battery swap.
  • Change lenses and cards between displays, never during one. Display teams and fast jets arrive with very little warning.
  • Keep spare batteries in an inside pocket on cold days. They last noticeably longer when they stay warm.

The frame rate rule

For flying displays I would advise a camera that shoots six frames per second or more. A Red Arrows cross or a Typhoon pass lasts a second or two, and more frames gives you more chances at the perfect moment.

That does not mean holding the shutter down for ten seconds. Short bursts of three or four frames as the aircraft hits the best spot works far better, and saves you hours of sorting later. I call it avoiding the machine gun.

If you are mainly shooting the static park or just want family memories, a slower camera is completely fine. Cameras under six frames per second are marked in red throughout this guide so you can see them at a glance.

DSLR cameras for aviation photography

DSLRs are still brilliant airshow cameras. They have optical viewfinders with no lag, huge battery life and grips made for long days. Most makers have now stopped developing new DSLRs, which is good news for you: many of the best aviation bodies ever made are now easy to find second hand at a fraction of their launch price.

What I shoot with

Mirrorless cameras for aviation photography

Mirrorless cameras are where all the new development is happening. The big win for airshows is subject detection: many current bodies will find an aircraft in the frame and stick to it, even a small jet against a busy sky. You also see your exposure in the viewfinder before you press the shutter, which helps a lot when you are learning.

Frame rate

Mirrorless speeds are the maker's headline figure and many are reached with the electronic shutter. With fast propellers, the electronic shutter can bend blades into odd shapes, so this may work better for jets than for warbirds.

Save money by buying second hand

You do not need a £5,000 camera to get great airshow photos. A used mirrorless body that was top of the range a few years ago can give you most of the performance for a fraction of the price. My own Z6 came second hand for around £400.

Buying second hand

Buying used is the single best way to get more camera for your money. Professionals upgrade often and sell kit that has years of life left in it. I picked up my Nikon Z6 second hand for around £400, and it films every airshow I go to.

I would suggest buying from a specialist dealer for your first used camera. You pay a little more than a private sale, but you get the camera checked, graded and covered by a warranty.

    Lenses for aviation photography

    Your lens matters more than your camera. For flying displays, I would advise at least 300mm on a crop sensor camera and 400 to 600mm on full frame. For the static park, a standard zoom or a small prime is all you need. You do not need the biggest lens out there. Shoot what your lens can reach, and fill the frame with it.

      Filming at airshows

      Filming an airshow is a different skill from photographing one. Smooth, steady footage with good sound will always beat shaky clips at the highest resolution. This section goes deeper into which gimbal to bring, and when a small pocket or action camera will do a better job.

      Please note

      Never bring a drone. Airshows sit inside restricted airspace while the displays are running, and organisers do not allow them on site. Your footage from the ground is what people want to see.

      Do you need a gimbal at all?

      A gimbal will work well when

      • You are walking the static park, the RAF Village or the trade stalls and want smooth moving shots.
      • You are filming people, such as a pilot meet and greet or your family reacting to a flypast.
      • You want slow reveal shots of aircraft on the ground, moving past a wing or along a fuselage.
      • You need a steady hold for a long display and cannot comfortably hold a camera still for that long.

      A gimbal may not suit you when

      • You are tracking fast jets with a long lens. Most handheld gimbals struggle with big lenses, and fast pans can fight the motors.
      • You are in a packed crowd line. A gimbal at arm's length takes space, so keep it close and be aware of people around you.
      • You only want short clips for social media. A phone or pocket camera with good stabilisation is lighter and quicker.
      • Your camera has strong in body stabilisation and you are filming from a tripod or monopod anyway.

      Gimbals worth bringing

      Pocket, action and 360 cameras

      Small cameras are perfect alongside a stills setup. Clip one to your bag strap, set it running for the display, and you have a second angle without thinking about it.

      Filming tips for airshows

      Pick 50 frames per second

      In the UK, 25 or 50 frames per second matches our mains power and avoids flicker under lights in hangars. Use 50 for smoother movement on fast passes.

      Use an ND filter

      A shutter speed of around double your frame rate gives natural looking movement. On a bright day you need an ND filter to get there, and it also helps propellers show as a disc rather than frozen blades.

      Beat the wind

      Airfields are windy. A furry windshield on your microphone makes a huge difference, and the sound of a Merlin or a Typhoon on afterburner is half the experience.

      Lock your settings

      Set focus tracking, white balance and exposure before the display starts. Auto white balance can shift as a jet passes through cloud.

      Hold the shot

      Let the aircraft fly in and out of frame rather than chasing it at the last second. Smooth, wide and steady will always cut together better.

      Carry power

      Gimbals, pocket cameras and action cameras drain quickly in cold or hot weather. A power bank and a short USB C cable will keep you going all day.

      Camera bags in all shapes and sizes

      There is no single perfect camera bag. I keep different bags for different days, and choose one around the show, my budget, the camera setup and the kind of photos I want. A small sling for statics and family memories, a backpack for a full two body day, and something with wheels when there is a long walk from the car park.

      Check first

      Some shows search bags at the gate or limit the size you can bring in. Check the show's own website before you set off.

      Cages and rigs for your camera

      A cage is a metal frame that fits closely around your camera body. It does two jobs. First, it protects the corners and screen from knocks, which matters when you are leaning on barriers or packing in a hurry. Second, it is covered in mounting points, so you can build a full filming rig around your camera.

      You can keep it simple and just add a cage for protection, or build it up with handles, a microphone and a monitor. SmallRig, Neewer and Tilta all make cages designed for specific camera models, so the battery door, card slot and ports stay easy to reach.

      Cage for protection

      • Adds very little weight and stays on all day.
      • Protects corners, the screen hinge and the lens mount from knocks.
      • Gives you a cold shoe for a small microphone without using the hot shoe.
      • I would suggest this for most people who film a little and photograph a lot.

      Full filming rig

      • Top handle for low and high angle shots, and a much steadier hold.
      • Side handle with a record button, so your hands stay in a natural position.
      • Room for a microphone, a monitor, a light or an SSD.
      • Heavier and bulkier. This will work well for keen filmmakers, but can be too much on a hot day in a crowd.

      Building a rig, step by step

      Cage

      Buy the cage made for your exact camera model. A generic cage can block the battery door or the screen.

      Base plate

      Choose a cage with an Arca Swiss style base, so it clicks straight onto most tripod heads and gimbals without an extra plate.

      Top handle

      The most useful add on at an airshow. Perfect for holding the camera low past the static aircraft or steady for a long take.

      Microphone

      A small shotgun microphone on a cold shoe, with a furry windshield, will lift your sound more than any other upgrade.

      Monitor

      A five inch monitor makes focus and framing easier in bright sun, where the camera screen can be hard to see.

      Weigh it

      Weigh the finished rig before your first show. If you also use a gimbal, keep the total well inside the gimbal's payload.

      Before you buy a cage

      4 checks
      • Check that the cage still lets the screen flip and tilt the way you use it.
      • Look for NATO rails and threaded holes in the places you will actually mount things.
      • Leave room for a battery grip if you use one, or buy a cage that fits around it.
      • Remember a cage changes the balance on a gimbal. Rebalance it at home, not on the crowd line.

      Our view

      The best airshow kit is the kit you can carry all day and use without thinking. A used camera at six frames per second or more, a long zoom that fits your budget, two spare batteries and a bag that suits how you travel will get you photos you are proud of. Everything else is a bonus. Start light, learn what you miss, and add to it one piece at a time.
